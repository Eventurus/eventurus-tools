Scams in China: the five setups, and how to end them

Five situations account for almost every report, and each one has a moment where walking away still works. This is a page about that moment.

The answer Five setups What it costs Ending it Complaining Not a scam Afterwards Questions

The short answer

Most trips meet none of this. The ones that do meet it in five places.

Stop it early. Every setup below depends on you staying polite and staying put. The four sentences in the middle of this page end most of them.
Do not pay under protest. Money handed over at the table is treated as agreement. Offer to settle at the police station instead.

Crime of the sort visitors fear is rare. What happens instead is commercial, scripted, and concentrated in tourist districts and transport hubs.

The amounts are usually small enough to swallow and large enough to spoil a day: ¥370 for tea is the figure that recurs; a nightlife bill can run into thousands.

Treat it as an etiquette problem rather than a danger. The people running these setups are selling, not threatening. Being rude costs nothing here; being polite can cost ¥900.

For the emergencies that are not commercial, our emergency guide has the numbers.

The five setups

How each one starts, the line where you leave, and what it usually costs.

1. The student, the exhibition, the tea

A friendly approach in English near a landmark: a student who wants to practise, or an art student whose teacher is showing work nearby. The exhibition is two rooms, and the tea that follows is charged by the leaf. Bills of a few hundred yuan are routine, and the number arrives at the end.

Leave when: the destination is a teahouse, a gallery with no other visitors, or anywhere you are asked to go through a door rather than stay in the street. You are allowed to say no to the invitation and keep walking.

2. Transport from the airport or station

A driver approaches inside the terminal and quotes a fare, three times the real one. The variant is a licensed car with a meter the driver insists is broken, or a route that circles the ring road twice.

Leave when: the driver will not start the meter, or cannot show a licence card. Walk to the official taxi rank, or order through Didi where the fare is agreed before you get in.

3. Tickets, guides, and today's closure

“The museum is closed today. Come, I show you a better place.” It works because it is sometimes true: sites do close for maintenance and do sell out by timed slot. The other version is a paper ticket sold above face value outside the gate, or a guide offering to skip a queue that does not exist.

Leave when: the ticket is not sold at the gate window or an official mini-program, or the closure is claimed by someone with a tour to sell. A real closure is posted, and the staff do not negotiate.

4. The bar, and the person who suggested it

A contact met online proposes a drink somewhere quiet. The bar is small, the menu has no printed prices, and the bill arrives in the thousands. A second person joins, and the bill grows.

Leave when: you are asked to go to a venue you did not choose, or the menu has no prices. Pay for what you ordered at a price you can read, and ask for the police if the number is refused.

5. Help with money, and the better rate

Someone offers to help at an ATM, to break a note, or to change money above the bank's rate. In the ATM version the helper watches the PIN. In the exchange version the notes are counted twice, by two pairs of hands.

Leave when: anyone stands close enough to see the keypad, or offers a rate the bank does not. Use an ATM inside a bank branch, and exchange at a bank counter.

Five, not fifty. A report that fits none of them is usually a pricing disagreement.

How to end it in the moment

Four sentences, in English and in pinyin. Say them flatly and keep walking.

1. No, thank you. I am not interested.

Bù yòng le, xièxie. Wǒ bù gǎn xìngqù.

The first refusal is the one that works. Do not explain, do not apologise, and do not slow down.

2. Please use the meter.

Qǐng dǎ biǎo.

For a taxi. If the answer is no, get out before the car moves. Photograph the plate as you do it.

3. I will pay what the menu says.

Wǒ zhǐ àn càidān shàng de jiàgé fù.

For a bill that arrived higher than the menu. Ask for the printed menu again, and photograph both.

4. I am calling the police.

Wǒ yào bàojǐng.

Dial 110 and hold the phone up. This line ends most of these conversations within a minute, and the police do attend commercial disputes in tourist areas.

Then ask for the receipt. 发票 (fāpiào) is the official invoice, and asking for one is a normal, unremarkable request that also puts the transaction on the record. A venue that cannot produce one has told you something.

Save the four sentences on your phone before you land. Reading them aloud beats mangling them from memory.

How to complain afterwards

Three channels, and what each one is actually for.

No Chinese number is the usual blocker. Three routes around it: the hotel front desk, which will place the call and let you use its number as the contact; a written complaint to the local market regulator, whose email is published on the city government site; and the booking platform, which for hotels and attractions has more leverage than you do.

Keep these four things: a photograph of the menu or price list, the receipt or the payment record in WeChat or Alipay, the venue's name in Chinese, and the time. Without the Chinese name, a complaint goes nowhere.

Expect a reply within a week for 12315 cases with a receipt, and longer without one. Refunds are common when the evidence is a photograph of two different prices.

For card payments, your bank's chargeback route runs in parallel and on its own timetable. Our payment guide covers the transaction records WeChat Pay and Alipay keep.

What is not a scam

Four things that read as a trick to a first-time visitor and are not.

Bargaining

In markets and souvenir streets the first price is an opening, and walking away is part of the conversation. The price you agree is the price. This is not dishonesty; it is the format.

Service charges and tea fees

Sit-down restaurants add 10 to 15 per cent, sometimes labelled a service fee. In Guangdong a tea charge (茶位费) of ¥2 to ¥10 a head is standard and appears on the bill. Both should be printed, not announced.

Menu price, then a different total

If the bill exceeds the menu, that is a real problem with a real remedy, and it is a pricing dispute rather than a setup: 12315 handles it. If the difference is one dish you did not order, it is usually a language failure at the ordering stage.

Ordering by QR code

A code on the table opens a menu, you pay before or after. It looks improvised and is the standard system in most cities. If the code leads to a page asking for a bank password, that is not the restaurant's system.

If it already happened

Three situations, three different responses.

1

You paid, and the amount was small.

The fix: file with 12315 anyway if you have a receipt, and tell your bank. Under ¥200 the practical value is the record rather than the refund, which matters if the same venue appears in other complaints.

2

You want it on record for insurance.

The fix: ask the police for a written record at the station, not at the scene. Bring your passport and the payment record; this takes an afternoon and produces a document insurers accept.

3

Your passport or phone was taken.

The fix: 110 first, then your embassy for a replacement travel document, then the exit-entry bureau for the visa entry. Our emergency guide sets out the order.

Skip naming the venue online before you have the receipt. A place that knows you have published will stop answering.

Frequently Asked Questions

The questions people ask before and after.

How common are scams in China?
Most visitors meet none. The cases that reach forums cluster in five situations: the tea invitation, transport from an airport or station, tickets and guides outside a gate, nightlife, and unsolicited help with money. The same setups appear in most large cities, in tourist districts rather than everywhere.
What should I do if a teahouse hands me a bill for several hundred yuan?
Do not pay at the table. Ask for the price list, photograph the bill, and say you are calling the police. Payment under protest weakens a later complaint, so offer to settle at the police station instead. Reports of ¥370 for tea are common; larger bills are commoner in the bar version of the same setup.
Can I complain without a Chinese phone number?
The online 12315 platform asks for a mainland mobile number for verification, which is the usual block for visitors. The 12345 city hotline is reachable on +86 plus the area code, and hotel front desks will usually place the call and act as the contact. Written complaints through the local market regulator's published email also work, slowly.
Is a taxi without a meter always a scam?
At an airport or station, a driver who quotes a flat fare instead of starting the meter is either unlicensed or overcharging. Legitimate cars have a meter, a licence card on the dashboard and a plate you can photograph. Use the taxi rank, or order through Didi where the fare is fixed before you get in.
What is not a scam in China?
Bargaining in markets, a 10 to 15 per cent service charge at a sit-down restaurant, the tea fee of ¥2 to ¥10 a head in Guangdong, and ordering by QR code. Prices that differ from what you expected are usually a translation gap rather than a trick.
Someone said the museum is closed today and offered a tour instead. Is that real?
Check the site's own notice or the official booking page. Closures happen, but the claim is also the opening line of a detour to a shop. A real closure is posted at the gate you are standing at, and staff will not offer you an alternative tour.
Should I report a small loss?
Report it if you want the record, for insurance or for a card dispute. Under ¥200 the practical route is the platform you booked through, plus a note to 12345 if you want a paper trail. Police reports at 110 are taken seriously but cost an afternoon.
