Markets in Shanghai Worth Your Time

One market makes clothes to your measurements in days. One is a wholesale hall in managed decline. One sells vegetables under bilingual signs, with a farm restaurant upstairs. They are not interchangeable.

A market is the only place in the city where the price is a conversation rather than a label. That is either the reason to go or the reason to stay away, depending on your patience.

At a glance

  • South Bund Fabric Market, 399 Lujiabang Road: three floors of tailors, collection usually within days.
  • Roughly half the customers at the fabric market are foreign; English signage throughout.
  • Qipu Road has about 3,000 clothing stalls, and most merchants reported falling sales in 2026.
  • Qipu Road closes en masse in June to August and around Spring Festival.
  • Wuzhong Market on Wulumuqi Road has bilingual signs and a farm restaurant upstairs.
  • A tailored suit exported unused within 90 days can qualify for the departure tax refund.

Which markets are worth the trip?

Three markets earn the trip: the South Bund fabric market for clothes made to measure in days, Qipu Road for cheap fashion and a wholesale floor in decline, and a neighbourhood wet market for the city itself. Wuzhong Market is the easiest first one.

Three, for three different reasons

  • For clothes that fit: the South Bund Fabric Market on Lujiabang Road, three floors of tailors who measure you, cut, and deliver in a few days. Roughly half the customers are foreign, English signage is everywhere, and most shops have a story about a customer who flew in for a fitting.
  • For cheap fashion: Qipu Road, a wholesale district of ten buildings and some three thousand stalls. It is in serious decline and empty in certain months. Go for bargains and atmosphere, not for quality.
  • For the city itself: a wet market, where the produce is the point and the shopping is incidental. Wuzhong Market on Wulumuqi Road is the easiest first one, with bilingual signage on many stalls and a farm restaurant upstairs.
Skip: the tourist bazaars around Yuyuan unless you want fridge magnets. They are pleasant, air-conditioned and priced for visitors.

The fabric market: how custom tailoring actually works

Walk all three floors before you sit down, because quotes vary shop to shop and a suit can cost a third of an international brand's price. Ask for the fabric price and the tailoring price separately, get measured properly, and insist on a fitting. A rush order skips the step that matters.

The process, from door to delivery

  • Step 1, walk the floors: the building has three. Ground floor is denser and busier, upper floors are roomier and calmer. A suit here can cost a third of what an international brand charges for the same cloth and cut, but quotes still vary shop to shop, so compare before you sit down.
  • Step 2, choose fabric and price separately: ask what the cloth costs per metre and what the tailoring costs, rather than accepting one number. Shops that quote a single figure are usually building in a margin you can argue with.
  • Step 3, get measured: a proper tailor takes a dozen measurements and asks how you want the jacket to sit. Bring a photograph of the cut you want. The reference image does more work than any vocabulary you can learn on the plane.
  • Step 4, agree a fitting: one fitting is normal for a suit, and a second is reasonable if the first is close but not right. Rush orders next-day are common, but a rushed suit skips the fitting that makes the difference.
  • Step 5, pay and collect: a deposit up front, the balance on collection. Mobile payment works and cards usually do, though some smaller stalls prefer cash. Keep the receipt.
Timing: the market is busiest in the two months before Spring Festival and from August to October, when wedding parties arrive. A weekday morning gets you the older tailors rather than their assistants.

What a tailor will and will not fix

The shops are strong on structured jackets, trousers, coats and qipao, because they have made them thousands of times. They are weaker on complicated womenswear and on anything you cannot attend a fitting for. Ask to see a finished garment in your size.

Set expectations before the deposit

  • They are good at: structured jackets, trousers, shirts, coats and qipao. The shops have made these thousands of times, and the pattern blocks are already right.
  • They are weaker at: anything needing a fitting you cannot attend, and complicated womenswear with internal structure. A wedding dress is not a qipao with more fabric.
  • The honest test: ask to see a finished garment in your size, not a sample on a mannequin. Any shop that cannot show one is asking you to be the first.
  • If it goes wrong: the fitting is your protection. Refuse delivery if the shoulders are wrong, because no alteration fixes a shoulder line cut too narrow.

Qipu Road: what it is still good for

Ten buildings and roughly three thousand stalls, under two kilometres from the Bund, and in managed decline: 2026 surveys found more than nine in ten merchants reporting falling sales. Still worth it for cheap basics between September and November, when the stalls are open.

A declining district, honestly described

  • The scale: ten large buildings and some three thousand clothing shops, a count that dates from 2024, in about half a square kilometre and under two kilometres from the Bund. It was once the largest clothing wholesale district in east China.
  • The state of it: industry surveys in 2026 found more than nine in ten merchants reporting falling footfall and falling sales. Online retail and livestream selling have taken the wholesale trade that used to fill these corridors.
  • The seasonal trap: stalls close en masse in June to August, when owners are away designing and sourcing, and again in January and February around Spring Festival. Visitors who arrive then find shutters and conclude the place has closed.
  • Still worth it for: cheap basics, sample pieces and the spectacle of a wholesale floor. Come in the September to November window, and treat the first price as an opening bid.
Getting there: Tiantong Road on lines 10 and 12 puts you at the edge of the district. The stalls are a five-minute walk from the exit.

How to shop a wet market

Pick your own produce, hand it to the stallholder, and pay by scanning the code on the counter. Prices for the same vegetable vary by a third between stalls, so one lap before buying is normal. Mornings are freshest and the last hour is cheapest.

The etiquette is simpler than it looks

  • Pick, point, pay: choose your own produce rather than asking an assistant, hand it to the stallholder, and pay by scanning the QR code on the counter. Weighing happens at the stall.
  • Browse first: prices for the same vegetable vary between stalls by a third. One lap before you buy is normal local behaviour, not rudeness.
  • Timing: mornings are freshest and busiest, and prices soften in the last hour before closing. Many stalls shut by early evening.
  • What to buy: fruit in season, dried goods, tea, and prepared foods sold hot. Anything you cannot refrigerate or carry home is a souvenir for the hotel room only.
  • Wuzhong Market: two floors on Wulumuqi Road, bilingual signs on many stalls, imported ingredients alongside local vegetables, and a Yunnan farm restaurant on the second floor that makes it the easiest market for a first visit.

Practical notes that save a wasted trip

Mobile payment covers almost everything, but carry a few hundred yuan in small notes for older stallholders. Bargain in clothing markets, never in wet markets. And if you plan to claim the departure refund on a tailored suit, do not wear it before you fly.

Before you go

  • Cash or phone: mobile payment is accepted almost everywhere, but a few older stallholders only take cash. Carry a couple of hundred yuan in small notes.
  • Bargaining: expected in clothing markets, not in wet markets. In a wholesale hall, walking away is a legitimate negotiating move. In a vegetable market it would be odd.
  • Tax refund on tailoring: a suit bought and exported unused within ninety days can qualify for the departure refund if the shop is registered. The refund guide explains the conditions, and the key one is that you must not wear it before you leave.
  • Shipping home: tailors will post garments abroad, and the cost is usually quoted at the shop. Ask for the tracking number before you pay the balance, not after.

Frequently asked questions

What people ask before setting out, answered first.

How long does a custom suit take in Shanghai?

Collection usually takes a few days, and next-day rush orders are common at the South Bund fabric market. A proper fitting adds a day, and skipping it is the most common reason a cheap suit looks cheap.

Do I need to speak Chinese to use the fabric market?

No. Most shops have English signage, many owners negotiate in English, and a photograph of the cut you want communicates more than vocabulary. Bringing your measurements written down helps.

Is Qipu Road still worth visiting?

For cheap basics and the atmosphere of a wholesale floor, yes, between September and November. For quality clothing, no. Large parts of the district close in summer and around Spring Festival, which is why visitors sometimes find it deserted.

Can I claim a tax refund on a tailored suit?

Only if the shop is a registered refund shop, you export the suit unused within ninety days, and you have the invoice and refund form. Wearing it before departure disqualifies the claim, so collect it close to your flight.

Which wet market should a first-time visitor try?

Wuzhong Market on Wulumuqi Road. It has bilingual signs, two floors of produce and prepared food, and a farm restaurant upstairs, which makes it legible without much Chinese. Go in the morning.

Do Shanghai markets take foreign cards?

Larger stalls and shops usually do, and mobile payment linked to a foreign card works almost everywhere. Smaller wet-market stalls may only accept mobile payment or cash, so carry a few hundred yuan in small notes.
